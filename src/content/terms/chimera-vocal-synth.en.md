intro: "**Chimera Vocal Synth** (branded CHIMERA in the app) is a physical-modeling voice synthesizer by Jameson Hodge. Sound comes from simulated lungs, vocal folds or a syrinx, and a branched vocal tract — not from sliced recordings. The engine is written in Rust, compiled to WebAssembly for an AudioWorklet in the browser; the same code also runs natively as the `chimera` CLI. The repository is https://github.com/jamesondh/chimera-vocal-synth and the demo is https://chimera-vocal-synth.vercel.app/ . GitHub lists the repo as created on 5 November 2026; it had about 1,289 stars at the time of this scan.\n\nAccording to the repo, you can drag the tongue, lips, and jaw, speak from English text or IPA, sing with lyrics on a piano roll, beatbox from pads and a sequencer, and switch in songbird, seal, alligator, or elephant voices. Male, female, and child bodies blend continuously. The license on the repository is MIT.\n\nThis radar database already has **ElevenLabs Chimera**, which is a different product from a different company. explain: "## Why it matters\n\nMost singing synthesizers stitch recorded phonemes and then shift pitch. CHIMERA takes the other path: it models the airway as a network of tubes so vowels, consonants, and some animal voices come out of geometry and airflow. For teaching, vocal experiments, or an interactive explainer, that is more useful than a clip you can only listen to, because you can drag articulators and watch the tract and formants move.\n\nIt also ships as a complete browser app, not just a paper or a CLI. The MIT license, a public repo, and a demo you can open immediately are why the name is worth recording on its own. The name collides with ElevenLabs Chimera, so this page uses Chimera Vocal Synth as the term and names the author and repository.\n\n## Limits\n\nThe project itself says naturalness is still being tuned, and consonants, timing, and real vocal-fold motion are harder than held vowels. A secondary article recaps a project-side comparison in which Whisper word error stays much higher than on real speakers; that is not our own test. Star counts change; use the GitHub page at the time you look. This is an independent open-source project, not an ElevenLabs product, and you should not assume commercial dubbing stability or broad language coverage."
